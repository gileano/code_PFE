\title{Critical Analysis of Quantum-Inspired Evolutionary Algorithms\\
for Multi-Objective Optimization:\\
Taxonomy, Features, and Limitations}
\author{Olivian-Antonio G\^{\i}lea \and Adrian Florea}
\date{\today}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Quantum-inspired evolutionary algorithms (QIEAs) borrow the Q-bit
chromosome and rotation-gate operator from quantum mechanics to improve
classical evolutionary search, and have been applied to multi-objective
optimization (MOO) since at least 2006 -- yet that literature remains
sparse, scattered across domains, and, to our knowledge, has not been
surveyed on its own terms. This paper proposes a taxonomy of QIEA-for-MOO
methods along five axes (representation scheme, quantum-inspired operator
type, selection and diversity mechanism, hybridization with classical
MOEAs, and application domain), applies it in a systematic feature
comparison, and critically analyzes the field's limitations. We find
that the surveyed methods almost uniformly inherit NSGA-II's
crowding-distance selection unchanged, layer at most one further
quantum-inspired operator on top of the rotation gate (with no
interference-based operator at all), and are benchmarked against a
classical baseline but rarely against one another; the Q-bit
representation also lacks a standard decoding convention for continuous
problems, where its performance has been reported to degrade as the
number of decision variables grows. Across the field, reproducibility
gaps, weak benchmarking practice, and scalability claims tested at a
single problem size recur regardless of method category. We ground these
claims in an illustrative case study: a block-partitioned
quantum-circuit-optimization pipeline extending a published NSGA-II
framework with NSGA-III and SMS-EMOA as interchangeable per-block
optimizers. It shows, among other things, that an adaptive, per-run
hypervolume reference point can change a cross-algorithm ranking that
a shared reference point keeps stable, and how a correctness bug can
silently invalidate weeks of results. We close with open challenges,
including the as-yet untested combination of a Q-bit-represented QIEA
with a quantum-computing problem domain.
\end{abstract}
